# Bandmaker: teaching beginners to choose keys, chords and progressions

Research date: 31 October 2026. [Research #79](https://github.com/dcromp/music-os/issues/79), part of [map #64](https://github.com/dcromp/music-os/issues/64), informing [prototype #73](https://github.com/dcromp/music-os/issues/73). Research-only follow-up using `.agents/skills/research/SKILL.md`, including delegated background reading and evidence drafting.

## Verdict

**A useful songwriting scaffold, but not yet an evidenced beginner curriculum.** The founder's key-type → practical key → chords → sequences order can be retained. The strongest improvement is to make every stage audible, give a playable default, highlight a small working subset while preserving the full seven-chord reference, and teach one purposeful edit before expanding vocabulary. Treat this as a testable design recommendation; the located evidence does not show that this navigation order outperforms starting from a progression.

Keep qualified major/minor associations and the separate power-chord approach. Do not teach key letters as emotions, all diatonic chords as equally easy/interchangeable, or natural minor as the only legitimate minor-key material. Correct chord spelling and diminished voicing are necessary content checks, not proof of learning.

## Empirical evidence and its limits

The research supports hearing, comparing, and manipulating examples within guided instruction. It does **not** establish that selecting a key before a progression teaches beginner guitarists better than starting with a progression. The founder's preferred order is a reasonable design hypothesis to test, not a result demonstrated by the studies below. Searches found no directly relevant controlled comparison of these interface sequences and no empirical threshold establishing that seven chord choices or twelve key choices are intrinsically excessive.

### Aural tonal patterns and instrumental learning

[Grutzmacher (1987), *The Effect of Tonal Pattern Training on the Aural Perception, Reading Recognition, and Melodic Sight-reading Achievement of First-Year Instrumental Music Students*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.2307/3344959), randomly assigned 48 beginning instrumentalists to tonal-pattern instruction using harmonization/vocalization or notation-oriented technical instruction. After 14 weekly 30-minute lessons, the tonal-pattern group performed better on major/minor aural identification and melodic sight-reading; reading recognition did not differ significantly. This compares instructional bundles, not guitar websites or key-first versus progression-first navigation. Access: publisher abstract verified; full text restricted.

**Design inference:** attach major/minor labels to heard examples and offer optional humming or singing. The finding supports an audible tonal framework, but does not justify requiring theoretical decisions before playback.

[Baker and Green (2013), *Ear playing and aural development in the instrumental lesson*](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10123257/), compared conventional instruction with instruction incorporating playing from recordings in 16 matched pairs of instrumental students aged 10–14. Ear-playing participants had higher post-test means across assessed criteria. This must not be reported as every difference being statistically significant: figures in the [author manuscript](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10123257/1/Green_ABRSM%20test%20article%20D10%20FINAL%20in%20WORD%2018%20Sep%2013.pdf) explicitly identify nonsignificant outcomes for contour, tempo, and overall grade. The small case-control sample, teacher-mediated instruction, and aural test closely aligned with the intervention limit generalization. Access: author manuscript inspected.

**Design inference:** include listening, imitation, and changing one element before replaying. This supports an aural component, not unguided discovery or a universal progression-first rule.

### Coordinating instructional information

[Owens and Sweller (2008), *Cognitive load theory and music instruction*](https://doi.org/10.1080/01443410701369146), reports two experiments. Integrated text and notation, and auditory explanation with notation, outperformed separated visual materials in the first; simultaneous instructional sources outperformed successive presentation in the second. Access: public publisher abstract verified; full methods were not accessible in this research pass, so population and effect-size claims are not made here.

**Design inference:** keep the active chord, short explanation, and playback together. This supports coordinating information, not a scientifically fixed choice-count limit. Sound-oriented teaching also need not mean temporally separating labels from sound. Showing fewer initial choices is a product hypothesis about relevance and learners' decision criteria, not an application of a proven seven-item or twelve-item threshold.

### Major/minor is one cue to emotion

[Eerola, Friberg and Bresin (2013), *Emotional expression in music: contribution, linearity, and additivity of primary musical cues*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00487/full), studied 46 participants in Stockholm and Jyväskylä rating 200 systematically varied examples from four musical structures for happiness, sadness, peacefulness, and scariness. Mode, tempo, register, dynamics, articulation, and timbre all contributed. Mode ranked highest overall, but rankings varied by emotion. These were perceived-emotion judgments under constrained stimuli, not guarantees about felt emotion across styles. Access: full article inspected.

**Design inference:** major/minor is a defensible starting cue, but rhythm and sound deserve audible treatment too. Compare different renderings of the same harmony; avoid presenting key type as a complete mood recipe.

[Smit et al. (2022), *Emotional responses in Papua New Guinea show negligible evidence for a universal effect of major versus minor music*](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0269597), studied 170 PNG participants with differing exposure to Western-influenced music, plus 60 Sydney nonmusicians and 19 musicians. Major cadences elicited greater reported happiness in all groups except the minimally exposed PNG community. The authors associate major/minor valence with cultural exposure and explicitly do not exclude universality. Happiness judgments do not independently validate descriptors such as “open,” “reflective,” or “tense.” Access: full article inspected.

**Design inference:** describe major/minor feelings as tendencies in familiar musical styles and invite learners to listen for themselves. Founder-preferred adjectives can be editorial listening prompts; they should not appear to be experimentally validated categories or deterministic rules.

### Tonic and function depend on context

[Krumhansl and Kessler (1982), *Tracing the Dynamic Changes in Perceived Tonal Organization in a Spatial Representation of Musical Keys*](https://static1.1.sqspcdn.com/static/f/221758/23618809/1380667475290/KrumhanslKessler1982TracingPerceviedTonalOrg-SpatialRep-Keys.pdf), uses probe-tone ratings after scales, chords, cadences, and chord sequences to investigate tonal hierarchies. Listeners integrate several chords when forming a sense of key; the prevailing key is not merely the most recent chord. This is perceptual research, not a beginner teaching intervention. Access: original paper PDF abstract inspected; no population-specific teaching claim is inferred.

**Design inference:** teach tonic through a short sequence and an audible point of rest. A seven-chord list supplies an inventory, not an explanation of key. Avoid implying that every ordering of the same chord set establishes the same perceived tonic.

## Evidence gaps to preserve in decisions

- No direct controlled comparison of Bandmaker's proposed navigation orders was found in this research pass.
- No established seven-chord or twelve-key cognitive threshold was found. Difficulty depends on what learners must understand and do with the choices.
- Child instrumental teaching studies do not directly establish effects for adult beginner guitarists using a self-guided app.
- Perceived emotion, enjoyment, conceptual understanding, independent composition, and physical guitar playability are distinct outcomes.
- A short successful builder session does not establish transfer to choosing chords independently or maintaining learning over time.
- The progressive disclosure, copy, and journey recommendations are design judgments informed by this evidence; they need observation with actual beginners.

## First-party teaching and theory: what they do and do not establish

These are teaching precedents and theory explanations, not controlled evidence that a particular onboarding interface works.

- **Andy Guitar, beginner electric Level 1:** applies E/A chords, power chords, single notes and rhythm to jam tracks and songs; the practice plan separates chord changes, rhythm and songs. This supports a practical alternative to requiring a complete key vocabulary before making music. It does not compare key-first with sound-first instruction experimentally. [First-party lesson](https://www.andyguitar.co.uk/videos/practice-routine-songs-for-level-1-beginner-electric).
- **JustinGuitar:** the indexed Grade 1 outline starts with A/D and a first song; the official beginner songbook follows the course's progression. This is a staged repertoire precedent, not proof that A/D is optimal for all learners. The course page supplied little extractable text; the sequence was checked against its indexed outline and official course category, not an inspected full video lesson. [Course](https://www.justinguitar.com/classes/beginner-guitar-course-grade-one), [official module category](https://community.justinguitar.com/c/beginner-guitar-course-grade-one/a-d-chords-your-first-song/136), [songbook](https://www.justinguitar.com/store/beginner-songbook-1).
- **Fender:** a power chord contains root and fifth, with optional octave duplication. Its absent third justifies keeping this as a separate approach rather than a third key type. It need not sound heavy when played softly and cleanly. [Power-chord lesson](https://www.fender.com/articles/chords/what-is-a-power-chord).
- **University of Puget Sound:** minor-key harmony uses variable sixth/seventh degrees; a natural-minor set is not the complete minor-key vocabulary. B7 in E minor is an ordinary dominant seventh using D♯, not necessarily a borrowed chord from another key. [Diatonic chords in minor](https://musictheory.pugetsound.edu/mt21c/DiatonicChordsInMinor.html). Reserve “borrowed” for a demonstrated relationship such as C minor replacing C major in G major, borrowing iv from parallel G minor. [Open Music Theory: modal mixture](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/modal-mixture/).
- **Berklee / Gabrielle Goodman:** singing fit depends on the song's melody range and the singer's comfortable notes; someone does not have one best key for all songs. Its lesson recommends trying different keys and allows changing an isolated troublesome melody note. Our proposal adds checking the whole verse/chorus, repeated high notes and sustained notes before committing a guitar take. [First-party vocal lesson](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/how-to-find-your-vocal-range-for-singing-pop-and-rb/).
- **Fender:** a capo raises open-string pitch and permits familiar open shapes in other sounding keys. For example G shapes + capo 2 gives sounding A major. It cannot lower the same no-capo setup. Show shape names and sounding chords separately; the same shape sequence becomes A–D–E–A in that example. [Capo explanation](https://www.fender.com/articles/parts-and-accessories/what-is-a-capo).

## Audit of the actual flow

The live URL is [Bandmaker](https://dcromp.github.io/music-os/bandmaker.html); browser-text retrieval failed, so live equivalence is supported only by the prior deployment record in [#73](https://github.com/dcromp/music-os/issues/73#issuecomment-6548775969).

| Observed behavior | Assessment / proposed response |
|---|---|
| Major/minor cards say “Often…” and warn that rhythm, melody and playing style matter | Keep the qualified associations. Add Hear at this decision; currently the cards themselves supply no sound. Avoid turning “often” into a grading rubric for the learner's feelings. |
| A selected family exposes a 12-key selector, seven triads and two sequences | There is a default, so the app does not literally require twelve separate decisions. But it gives a novice little basis to override it. Keep a recommended key prominent and put all keys behind Change key. The issue is unfamiliar choices plus missing consequences, not a proven maximum of twelve. |
| All seven diatonic triads, including diminished, have equal prominence | Correct reference material can still be a poor first practice menu. G major includes Bm and F♯dim; C major includes F and Bdim. “Open-chord key” does not mean every chord in its full palette is an easy open shape. Highlight the recipe's two to four chords and retain All seven. |
| Two transposed recipes can be heard and used; bars can be edited, repeated, returned home and undone | Strong basis for learning through making. Teach one audible edit and ask what changed. A novice completing a preset is not yet evidence they understand key or can choose another progression. |
| Natural-minor collection is named; the V7 example explicitly says raised seventh outside natural minor | Accurate and worth preserving. Add “a common sound in minor-key music” so “outside natural minor” is not mistaken for “wrong in this key.” Do not automatically call it borrowed. |
| Named shapes exist for a small subset; other chords get synthesized pitches | Transposition is not a guitar fingering solution. G–C–D–G becoming F♯–B–C♯–F♯ preserves relationships but can raise fretting difficulty. Require shape/transition guidance before calling a transposed recipe playable. Synthesized voicing changes can also confound perceived key differences. |
| The build view says standard tuning/no capo and has a singing disclosure | Good boundaries. Keep the key provisional until a melody exists; capo and actual guitar setup are future guidance, not current functionality. |
| Current recipe pairs change rhythm as well as chords; major/minor families also change tempo/tone | Fine as whole musical ideas. They cannot teach that the different feeling was caused only by major/minor or a particular chord. Add dedicated one-change listening examples when explaining cause. |

## Decision: retain key-first structure, make each step audible and provisional

**Design judgment, not a proven learning superiority:** keep the founder's visible order **key type → practical key → chords → suggested sequences**, but make listening available from the first card, automatically recommend a setup, and delay full theory until a learner has made a musical choice. This is a refinement of the founder's preference, not an instruction to revert to the superseded feel-first interface.

Key-first has genuine advantages: it exposes the organizing idea, makes transposition understandable, and supports a learner deliberately wanting a harmonic framework. Its risk is teaching labels and a chord inventory before the learner can hear what they organize. Sound/progression-first gives an immediately meaningful object to copy/edit; its risk is preset browsing without transferable understanding. The proposed hybrid makes each label answer an audible question, then tests transfer. No located study settles which of these exact flows works better for adult beginner guitarists on phones.

### Proposed first visit and finished example copy

All choices and numerical limits here are hypotheses for testing, not evidence-derived thresholds.

1. **Choose a family and hear it.** Retain the founder's three cards, with short previews and replay, no automatic audio. Copy: **“Major — often bright, open or uplifting.” “Minor — often reflective, melancholy or tense.” “Power chords — a separate approach using root and fifth.”** Shared line: **“These are common associations, not rules. Listen and choose what fits your song.”** Offer “Not sure? Try a starting idea.” For an explicit major/minor explanation, compare the same tonic, rhythm, register and tone, e.g. A–D–E–A against Am–Dm–Em–Am. Label these listening examples; matching all guitar voicings perfectly is not assumed. The current six recipes are whole arrangements, not controlled mode comparisons.
2. **Accept or change a practical key.** Copy: **“Start in G major. This example uses G, C and D. Know those shapes? Start here, or choose shapes you already play.”** Optional chips: “I know a few chords”, “New to chords”, “Just listening”. Keep this skippable. A learner who knows A/D/E gets A major, not a mandatory G start. For the minor route propose Em/Am first if those are familiar; for power chords use E5/A5 before adding movable shapes. “New to chords” needs a chord-shape demonstration and a one-chord pulse task; this helper alone does not teach fretting technique. All twelve keys remain in Change key, with explicit setup consequences rather than invented difficulty rankings.
3. **Show the working chord family.** Copy: **“Start with these chords in G major: G · C · D. G is our home chord. Hear each one, then hear them together.”** Beneath: **“See all seven chords from the G major scale.”** This still satisfies “show chords in the key” while distinguishing starter subset from complete reference. If the founder insists on all seven visible, highlight the three used now and label the others “Explore later”; test that version rather than silently changing the requirement.
4. **Choose a sequence, then make one edit.** First example G–C–D–G; next G–D–Em–C. One chord per bar in 4/4, simple downstrokes, speed adjustable. Copy: **“A progression is chords placed in time. Try this one, then change one bar.”** Offer Hear / Use / Slower, show the current chord during playback, and keep Undo. Before introducing Em, show its shape and hear it against G. Do not call the second loop's tonal home inevitable without melody. For an earlier stage use G–C–G–G, or A–D–A–A for A/D learners; these are authored teaching exercises, not attributed songs.
5. **Experience home before naming function.** Compare G–C–D stopping there with G–C–D–G. Ask **“Which ending sounds more settled to you?”** Then explain **“We are treating G as home — musicians call that the tonic.”** It is an invitation to listen, not a mandatory emotional response. Later add I/IV/V alongside chord names and transpose A–D–E–A to show a relationship that survives new letters. Teach return, continuation and repetition; don't imply every rock loop must use V–I or end on tonic.
6. **Practise one manageable layer.** First keep a pulse on one chord; then change two shapes, one downstroke per bar; then try a complete loop and add a rhythm. Offer tempo lowering without labelling slow playing as failure. Power-chord fingering, muting unused strings and palm muting need demonstrations; fewer distinct notes do not guarantee easier motor coordination. The first achievement is a chosen, editable idea plus a playable fragment, not mastering seven chords.
7. **Change the arrangement and revisit the key.** Hear the same progression with ringing versus muted strokes, then slower/faster, then clean/drive with comparable playback level. Copy: **“Same chords, different delivery. Which suits your song?”** If singing: **“No melody yet? Keep this starting key. When you have a tune, try the whole verse and chorus and move the key if needed.”** If capo is introduced, explain both shapes and sound; avoid suggesting changing only the displayed key transposes recorded audio.

### Progressive disclosure and harmonic accuracy

| When | Show / teach | Concrete example and caution |
|---|---|---|
| First sound | Family, recommended setup, Hear, two-to-four used chords, four bars | Number of visible chords follows the learner/recipe, not a universal cognitive capacity. Keep advanced access discoverable. |
| First successful edit | Home; repeat/replace; duration; one new chord | G major can contain Em. A minor chord is not proof that the song is in a minor key. |
| Curious learner opens full family | Seven scale-built triads, ordered by scale degree; optional Roman numerals | G, Am, Bm, C, D, Em, F♯dim; E natural minor: Em, F♯dim, G, Am, Bm, C, D. This describes a note collection, not seven equally interchangeable chord functions. |
| Diminished selected | Three-note spelling, contextual listen, optional playable shape | F♯dim = F♯–A–C in both collections. In G major hear F♯dim–G; in E minor it is ii°, not automatically the same tonic-directed leading-tone role. Avoid “always resolves home” and distinguish a diminished triad from dim7. Correct rendering alone does not teach its use. |
| Minor return explored | One extra option with comparison | Em–Am–Bm–Em versus Em–Am–B7–Em, same tempo/rhythm. “B7 adds D♯, which can lead up to E.” Common minor vocabulary, outside natural minor; not forbidden. |
| Learner wants colour | One explained borrowing, then Undo | G–C–Cm–G versus G–C–C–G. “Cm borrows a sound from G minor.” Cm may be harder to fret and may conflict with a melody; listen first and show the actual shape. |
| Learner asks why / changes key | Chord function, degree patterns, shape versus sounding pitch | Roman numerals are a transfer aid after an audible example, not an entry quiz. More modes/secondary dominants/full reference can follow. |

The diminished, borrowing and function examples are music-theory demonstrations authored for this report. They have not been rendered or learner-tested. Minor theory support is the Puget Sound source above; modal borrowing support is the Open Music Theory chapter above. Avoid converting tonal-theory vocabulary into universal rules for all genres.

## Lightweight test before further product commitment

**Proposed study, not completed evidence.** Recruit 6–8 adult beginner guitarists with a spread from no chords to a few open shapes; record experience, familiar shapes, musical background and singing intention. Include people beyond the founder. Use their normal phone and guitar where feasible; compensate consistently. Keep private recordings/notes outside tracked source, with consent.

1. **Twenty-five to thirty minutes each.** First observe 3–5 minutes in the current flow without explaining the intended model. Ask them to make a short idea they like, choose a starting key, hear it, change one bar, undo, then attempt a manageable guitar fragment. Record first-sound time, stalls, requests for help, arbitrary key selection, and unplayable choices. Distinguish interface discovery from fretting limitations.
2. **Compare three low-fidelity variants:** A current key-first/all-seven; B proposed audible key-first/small highlighted subset; C progression-first followed by naming its key/family. Use the same audio, controls and amount of teaching text where possible, and counterbalance order. A→B bundles improvements; it cannot isolate ordering. The more informative B↔C comparison should differ mainly in ordering. Six participants permit all six orderings once; 6–8 is formative, not a powered efficacy trial. Use equally difficult but different examples to reduce memorization; document residual carryover.
3. **Measure learning separately from preference.** Before/after and an optional 48–72-hour follow-up, ask in their own words: what is home; can a major-key loop use a minor chord; why might you change key; does a capo change minor to major; are the seven shown chords the only ones allowed? Ask them to choose between a new easy/hard setup using known shapes, and edit an unfamiliar loop while explaining the change. Accept ordinary language; no Roman-numeral quiz is needed. Capture wrong ideas rather than coaching until after the task.
4. **Listen and play separately.** Ask “Which fits the sound you want?” before displaying mood labels for a separate pair; allow neither. For musical explanations, hold other cues fixed. Observe whether chord changes are feasible at a self-selected slow tempo; do not infer performance skill from successful button presses. Have a guitar teacher inspect demonstrations and diminished/borrowed shapes before testing those lessons.
5. **Predeclare a practical decision rule:** as an initial product gate, aim for at least 6 of 8 learners to reach an editable loop without facilitator rescue and explain a practical key choice, with no recurring false rule (“major always happy”, “all seven required”, “outside scale means wrong”). This is a proposed iteration target, not a statistical confidence claim. Report counts and individual failures, not significance. Prefer the variant with better transfer and fewer misconceptions; speed and liking alone cannot establish teaching quality. If results split by skill, offer a guided start and an exploration/reference mode.

## Remaining gaps and next action

No study located tests this exact flow, this population, this synthesizer, these chord counts or these phone layouts. The emotion studies concern perceived expression under particular conditions, not guaranteed feelings or songwriting skill. Existing recipe audio has not been auditioned in this research. Guitar shape ergonomics, accessibility, screen-reader announcements, small-screen visibility, preview fidelity and long-term retention remain untested. No exact optimal key count, chord count or lesson length is established.

**Recommended next action for #73:** prototype the audible key-first variant and a matched progression-first comparison only after the founder chooses to act on this report; then run the formative test. Retain the current deployed flow until that separate product work is authorized. Research completion does not accept the app as an effective curriculum or resolve #73/#64.
