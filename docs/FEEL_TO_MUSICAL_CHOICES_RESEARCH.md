# From intended feeling to musical choices

Research completed 30 October 2026 for [research #77](https://github.com/dcromp/music-os/issues/77), child of [Band mode map #64](https://github.com/dcromp/music-os/issues/64); informs [prototype #73](https://github.com/dcromp/music-os/issues/73). Produced by a background researcher under `.agents/skills/research/SKILL.md`. Research and proposed teaching material only: no prototype change, audio audition, deployment or founder acceptance is claimed.

## Recommendation

Keep the agreed **feel → suggested key → chord choices → progression** flow, with two short examples immediately after the feel choice. Treat each example as an editable musical starting point, not an emotion detector or a guarantee. Choose the *colour and arrangement* to explore the intended feeling; choose the *tonic/key name* for playable guitar shapes and, when known, the singer's melody.

Start with three invitations: **Happy / uplifting**, **Sad / reflective**, and **Heavy / aggressive**, plus **Explore without a label**. Recommend G major for the first, E minor for the second, and an E-centred power-chord arrangement for the third. These are proposed practical defaults, not findings that G is inherently happy or E inherently sad/heavy. Use familiar shapes, standard tuning and no capo initially. Offer two four-bar recipes per invitation; give the player a clear reason, an easier practice version and permission to change every choice.

A beginner without a melody or known singing range can start instrumentally now. Label the key **Starting key — change later**. A future vocal melody may require transposition or rewriting before committing guitar takes. Do not guess a singer's key from a mood, gender, voice label or one hummed note.

## Scope, provenance and evidence language

Local inputs are the founder agreement in `docs/BAND_MODE_STRUCTURE.md`, the preserved text in `docs/reference/SONG_CHORDS_USER_REFERENCE.md`, its separate `SONG_CHORDS_REFERENCE_NOTES.md`, README status and the root glossary. The unnamed book and its named-song claims remain unverified. They are not empirical evidence or verified recording analyses. The existing Foundation helper offers major-key palettes and per-bar progressions; it does not yet implement minor-mode teaching. None of the recipes below should be represented as already available.

This document uses three distinct kinds of support:

- **Empirical:** an original study observed a response in particular listeners and stimuli. This supports a tendency within those conditions, not a universal recipe.
- **Theory / pedagogy:** first-party teaching explains a musical relationship or technique. It does not establish its emotional effect experimentally.
- **Design proposal / subjective tendency:** our suggested defaults, wording, recipes and evaluation plan. These need listening and beginner-use checks; numerical tempos are authored examples, not scientifically derived emotional thresholds.

“Sounds sad” is **perceived expression**; “makes me sad” is **felt emotion**. Ask primarily whether an example fits the sound the player intends. Preference, familiarity, personal associations and playing ability are separate outcomes.

| [Smit et al. ## Teach three different decisions

### 1. Key: choose a home and a playable location

Explain: **“The key gives the music a home. Major or minor changes the colour; the letter helps us put it somewhere comfortable to play and sing.”** This is a teaching simplification: some loops have ambiguous homes, and a power-chord riff alone need not determine major/minor.

Suggested decision order:

1. If a melody exists, hear its whole phrase and comfortable low/high notes; try nearby keys while preserving its contour. Include where most notes sit, not only the extremes. A chorus can reveal a problem that the verse misses. Berklee's first-party vocal lesson explicitly discusses upper/lower comfort, transposing and occasionally changing an isolated troublesome note. [Berklee / Gabrielle Goodman](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/how-to-find-your-vocal-range-for-singing-pop-and-rb/)
2. If no melody exists, use the guitar defaults below. Let the player hum freely over the loop, then revisit the key. No range test is a prerequisite to exploring chords. This is our proposed low-friction teaching sequence.
3. Prefer shapes the player already knows; otherwise offer **open chords** or **two-note power chords**. “Easy key” means an easy *arrangement for this person*, not a property of a key signature.
4. Offer capo only when useful. A capo raises the sounding pitch while retaining familiar shapes; it does not turn major into minor. Show both identities, for example **G shapes + capo 2 → sounding A major**. To lower a no-capo arrangement, choose different shapes or another setup; a capo cannot lower those same open strings. Fender's own guide explains capo transposition and the retained open-shape sound. [Fender, What Is a Capo?](https://www.fender.com/articles/parts-and-accessories/what-is-a-capo)

**No melody / no range default:** G major with G–C–D–Em available; E minor with Em–C–Am–D initially available; E-centred power shapes with E5–G5–A5–C5–D5 available. Keep the initial screen to the chords used by the selected recipe. A player who only knows A/D/E should be offered an A-major arrangement instead of being forced into G. Do not silently transpose recorded audio when changing the guide.

### 2. Palette: offer a small family, explain one useful contrast

Theory facts below come from [Puget Sound, Diatonic Chords in Minor](https://musictheory.pugetsound.edu/mt21c/DiatonicChordsInMinor.html), [Open Music Theory, Modal Schemas](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/modal-schemas/), and [Fender, Power Chords](https://www.fender.com//articles/chords/what-is-a-power-chord). Their emotional descriptions in the table are our audition prompts, not tested outcomes.

| Family | Proposed beginner palette | What to explain / defer |
|---|---|---|
| Major starting point | G, C, D; reveal Em when the second example uses it | “These give us home, movement and a way back; Em adds a contrasting minor chord.” A major-key song can contain minor chords. The complete diatonic set is larger; this is a deliberate subset. |
| Natural-minor starting point | Em, C, Am; reveal D for another loop | Relative to E: i, ♭VI, iv, ♭VII. Keep E as an intended home through returns, duration and later melody. G major and E natural minor share a note collection; the home is not established merely by listing those notes. |
| Minor with a stronger return | Add B7 to Em, Am | B7 includes D♯, the raised seventh leading into E. Explain “a more pointed return” and audition it; it is not a promise of more sadness. It lies outside the E natural-minor collection, but is ordinary minor-key vocabulary. |
| Power-chord starting point | E5 with G5/A5, or C5/D5 | Root and fifth, optionally doubled at the octave; no third defining major/minor. Roots alone do not settle the mode. Distinguish E5 from E major or Em in labels and audio. |

Keep modal colour behind **Try another colour**, with one demonstrated change at a time. Dorian can be introduced as a minor tonic with a raised sixth: compare **Am–Dm** and **Am–D**, holding the other conditions constant. Mixolydian has a major tonic and lowered seventh: **A–G–D–A** can supply that colour. These examples suggest modal material without proving the entire song stays in a mode. Lydian (raised fourth) and Phrygian (lowered second) can wait until the player asks for more; do not give seven unexplained mode names on the first screen. Modal “bright”, “dark” or “floating” descriptions remain subjective prompts. [Open Music Theory, Modal Schemas](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/modal-schemas/)

Do not inherit the saved reference's mistakes: B diminished, not B♭, is the natural-minor ii° in A minor; a major II or III is not automatically a secondary dominant; extensions and borrowed chords are not always interchangeable under a melody. Keep its original text intact and use its correction notes.

### 3. Progression: make time, return and repetition audible

Teach a palette as the available ingredients and a progression as their order **and duration**. Let the player swap a single bar, repeat a chord, or finish on home. “Home → move away → return” is useful shorthand for some patterns, not a rule that all pop follows: rock uses modal and plagal patterns as well as conventional dominant returns. [Open Music Theory, Harmony in pop/rock](https://openmusictheory.github.io/popRockHarmony.html)

Starting on a minor chord does not by itself make a loop unambiguously minor or sad. Some four-chord schemas admit relative-major and relative-minor hearings, affected by melody and context. [Open Music Theory, Four-Chord Schemas](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheorycopy/chapter/4-chord-schemas/)

The beginner controls should be **keep / swap this chord**, **repeat**, **return home**, **slower / faster**, and **ring / mute**. Keep Roman numerals as optional explanatory detail. Do not imply that every ordering in a palette communicates the same thing, or that only four-chord songs are legitimate.

## Six original example recipes for a later listening comparison

These are newly specified teaching arrangements using common harmonic vocabulary, not transcriptions or claims of musical uniqueness. They have not been rendered or auditioned. Proposed descriptive names are listening invitations. All use **standard E–A–D–G–B–E tuning, no capo, 4/4, one chord per bar, four bars**. Play once for an initial short comparison, with optional looping. Quarter-note BPM is explicit; exact speeds and sounds are design choices.

| ID and invitation | Bars 1 → 4 | Intended home / degree pattern | Suggested performance | Practical explanation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| H1 — Open and buoyant | G · C · D · G | G major; I–IV–V–I | 108 BPM, clean ringing guitar, quarter-note down-strums, gentle accents on beats 2 and 4 | Three open shapes, an audible departure and return. G is chosen for the shapes. |
| H2 — Lift with contrast | G · D · Em · C | G-major starting interpretation; I–V–vi–IV | 108 BPM, same clean sound; straight eighths with lighter up-strokes | One minor chord within the major palette; the loop continues rather than ending on G. The home can remain ambiguous without melody. |
| S1 — Quiet reflection | Em · C · Am · Em | E minor; i–♭VI–iv–i | 72 BPM, clean guitar, one soft down-strum per bar, let ring | Open shapes and space; returns to Em. Reflective is an invitation, not a property of E. |
| S2 — Pull back home | Em · Am · B7 · Em | E minor; i–iv–V7–i | 72 BPM, same clean sound; root/upper-string plucks on beats 1/3 | B7 changes the colour and leads into Em; teach its shape as an optional next step. Use S1 if that transition interrupts playing. |
| R1 — Stomping riff | E5 · G5 · A5 · E5 | E-centred; root degrees 1–♭3–4–1 | 96 BPM, moderate drive, quarter-note down-strokes, palm-muted beats 1–3, open beat 4 | Low E and movable two-note shapes; no third, so do not label this E minor by itself. |
| R2 — Driving climb home | E5 · C5 · D5 · E5 | E-centred; root degrees 1–♭6–♭7–1 | 120 BPM, same drive, straight eighth-note down-strokes, mute all except beat 1 of each bar | Different root motion and greater attack density. It draws on minor-coloured roots but the omitted thirds leave harmonic interpretation open. |

For R2, reduce to quarter notes or single low root notes at 72–96 BPM as a practice version. Clearly label a single-note reduction as such. Hearing an energetic arrangement should not require immediately playing its full speed.

**Playable voicings for future rendering/checking.** From low E to high E, `x` means do not sound: G `320003`, C `x32010`, D `xx0232`, Em `022000`, Am `x02210`, B7 `x21202`; two-note E5 `02xxxx`, G5 `35xxxx`, A5 `x02xxx`, C5 `x35xxx`, D5 `x57xxx`. These are authored choices verified by standard-tuning pitch arithmetic, not a guarantee of a beginner's comfort. Keep fretting and unwanted-string muting visible. The power-chord route adds a right-hand muting skill: Fender teaches that palm position and pressure change ringing versus muted articulation. [Fender, Palm Muting](https://www.fender.com/articles/techniques/3-keys-to-ace-your-palm-muting)

Full four-bar durations, before a tail, are approximately 8.89 seconds for H1/H2, 13.33 seconds for S1/S2, 10 seconds for R1 and 8 seconds for R2. These follow `16 beats × 60 / BPM`. Avoid a melody in the first render so the later singer can create one, but acknowledge that harmonic-only examples leave tonal and emotional information unresolved.

## Bounded teaching flow and copy

1. **Feel.** “How would you like this to sound?” Show the three invitations and Explore. Accept the player's own words; do not diagnose their present mood.
2. **Hear two starts.** Offer the relevant pair above. “Which is closer — A, B, neither?” A replay is more useful than requiring theory vocabulary. Show the tempo and technique difference without suggesting the comparison isolates one cause.
3. **Suggested key.** “Try G major: these open shapes give you a practical place to start. The major colour and steady rhythm are one way to explore an uplifting sound.” For reflection: “Try E minor: Em, C and Am use open shapes. Space and a softer attack help explore this quieter version.” For heavy: “Start around E with power chords: the low open E supports the riff. Muting, rhythm and drive do much of the work.” Include Change key and “Planning to sing? We can check the key once you have a melody.”
4. **Chord choices.** Highlight only the selected recipe's three or four chords, each playable on its own. One optional colour at a time. Keep a route to the full palette; do not remove advanced control.
5. **Progression.** Load four editable bars. Let the player swap one, repeat, or extend. Provide Undo/Reset so experimentation has a clear return point. Guide changes affect recommendations and future synthesized notes; audio-transposition behavior needs separate design.
6. **Make it yours.** Expose tempo, pulse/strum, ring/mute and tone as separate musical choices. Before recording a committed part, revisit singing comfort if a melody has emerged. Band members remain freely accessible; this helper is not a mandatory project workflow.

Proposed first-use limits: three invitations, two previews per invitation, three or four visible chords, four bars, one optional colour, three initial tone descriptions (**clean / soft clean / driven**). G/Em/E-centred are defaults, not a restricted key list. Retain arbitrary edits behind Change/More; do not surface all 15 key spellings and all reference columns before the first sound.

## Reproducible later audio and beginner comparison plan

**No audio or prototype was changed for this research.** The six table entries define a brief for the next approved prototype task, not accepted musical assets.

1. Render each recipe from recorded parameter data: recipe/version, sounding notes and octaves from the voicings, tuning/capo, BPM, 4/4, bar boundaries, stroke onset times/direction, velocities, articulation/release, instrument/amp preset and gain. Fix the sound-library version and any randomness. Preserve this manifest with each original render.
2. Use actual guitar recordings or a credible guitar sound for judging shape, strum and driven tone. The present synthesized triads can check chord order but cannot establish palm-muting realism or heavy feel. Verify correct notes, no unintended strings, no clipping, exact four-bar timing and a clean loop boundary. Have a capable player/listener audit the result; file integrity is not musical acceptance.
3. Make a **recipe comparison** first: H1/H2, S1/S2, R1/R2, with neutral A/B names, randomized order, replay and “neither”. Keep perceived playback loudness comparable and record the matching method/settings. These pairs deliberately vary multiple features; preference cannot identify which feature caused it.
4. Add separate **one-change comparisons** only to explain causes: same progression/voicing/tone at two tempos; same notes/tempo with ring versus mute; same performance with clean versus drive at matched playback level. For major/minor, use a dedicated same-tonic G–C–D–G versus Gm–Cm–Dm–Gm listening pair with matched timing/register/timbre; it need not be the beginner's playable arrangement. For Dorian colour compare Am–Dm–Am–Dm against Am–D–Am–D. These isolate the specified musical change more closely, but do not prove universal emotion effects.
5. Ask separately: “Which fits your intended sound?”, “Which do you prefer?”, “Can you hear the difference?”, “Can you comfortably play it?” and “What would you change?” Record free descriptions before showing evocative recipe names. Include unfamiliar/uncertain responses and the participant's musical familiarity.
6. Observe a beginner choosing, explaining and changing one bar, then trying the progression slowly. Record time to a usable start, mistaken key/shape/capo interpretations, chord-transition difficulty, and whether they think the app guarantees emotions. A small formative session tests usability and copy, not population-level emotional accuracy.

## Open decisions and next work

- **Founder listening choice:** Are the six rendered starts recognizably useful contrasts, and does “neither” lead somewhere helpful? No preferred loop or style is accepted by this research.
- **Beginner capability:** Which open chords does the intended first user already know? Should heavy begin with single-note roots or two-note power shapes? Default choices require an actual playing check.
- **Preview fidelity:** Choose guitar rendering/recording and a documented loudness method before evaluating heavy/ringing differences. Existing triad playback is insufficient evidence for these timbral recommendations.
- **Minor support:** Add an explicit minor/power-chord model in later work; do not approximate it by relabeling the current major-key table. Decide whether B7 is available in the first minor revision; defer Dorian/Mixolydian to optional comparisons if scope is tight.
- **Vocal fit:** Decide whether the first version uses a manual “too high / too low” trial or a later melody/range tool. Unknown range must not block instrumental creation.
- **Changing an existing project:** Specify how guide transposition, section keys, MIDI/synth parts and recorded takes relate. Never imply that changing a key label retunes an existing recording.
- **Language:** Happy/uplifting, sad/reflective and heavy/aggressive are broad paired invitations. If users distinguish their halves, split or rename them based on observation; do not expand into an unsupported emotion taxonomy now.

Research completion means the sources, distinctions and testable proposal are captured. Implementation, listening validation, learner testing and founder approval remain separate work.
