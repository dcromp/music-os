## Summary of the text (pages 46 to 55)

**Primary chords (columns 1 to 6).** These are the six chords that belong to each key. They work together in any order. I, IV and V are always major. II, III and VI are always minor. The table uses only plain major and minor forms, but you can play sevenths, suspensions and so on over the same roots. Arctic Monkeys' "Riot Van" (chords I to V in A) and the Eagles' "Take It Easy" (I, II, IV, V, VI) use only these chords.

**Flat-degree chords (columns 7 to 9).** ♭VII, ♭VI and ♭III are major chords built on lowered scale notes. They aren't strictly in the key, but they add a bluesy, harder edge.
- **♭VII** is very common because of blues harmony and the Mixolydian mode. You can use it to approach V (I-♭VII-V) or to approach I. The book contrasts C-Em-Am-F-G-C with C-Em-Am-F-B♭-C, which closes each vocal phrase in the verse of "Help."
- **♭III** is strongly blues-derived. It shows up as IV-♭III-I or I-♭III. Examples include Arctic Monkeys' first album, the White Stripes and the Black Keys.
- **♭VI** works in blues and rock, but also in lighter pop such as "Hello Goodbye" and "Honey Pie."
- **♭VI-♭VII-I** (A♭-B♭-C) is a common phrase ending. It approaches I without using V. A ♭VI that slides down a half step to VI also sounds very satisfying, as in Boston's "More Than a Feeling."

**Hard-rock formula.** Combine I, IV and V with the three flat-degree chords. In C:
- I-♭III-IV (C-E♭-F): "Back in the USSR," "My Sharona" (reversed)
- I-♭III-IV-V-♭VII (C-E♭-F-G-B♭): "Baby You're a Rich Man," "In the Midnight Hour"
- I-♭III-IV-♭VI-♭VII (C-E♭-F-A♭-B♭): "Hey Joe," "Trampled Underfoot"
- I-♭III-IV-V-♭VI-♭VII: "Brown Sugar"
- I-IV-♭VII (C-B♭-F): "Get Back," "Another Girl"
- I-IV-V-♭VII (C-F-G-B♭): "A Hard Day's Night," "The Jean Genie," "Pretty Vacant"
- I-V-♭VII (C-G-B♭): "I'm a Loser"; reversed as I-♭VII-V in "Misty Mountain Hop"

The White Stripes build much of their music on these chords. Flat-degree chords also work well for toughening one section so it contrasts with a gentler one. A rare example is I-♭VII-♭V-♭III in "California Über Alles."

**Mixolydian songs.** The Mixolydian scale is a major scale with a lowered 7th. That gives you ♭VII and a minor V. Examples: Foo Fighters' "Times Like These" and "Overdrive" (Am in D major), "Polly Comes Home," and "You Got the Love," which loops Vm-IV-I throughout.

**Reverse polarity (columns 10 to 12).** These flip a chord's quality. II and III become major, and IV becomes minor.
- **II maj** (D in C) briefly suggests a key change, then the move to IV cancels it. Playing it as D7 strengthens the effect. Examples: "All You Need Is Love," "Walk On," "Rockin' Chair."
- **III maj** (E in C) suggests a move toward A minor. E7 heightens it. Examples: "Golden Slumbers," "Innuendo." "Dancing Queen" uses both in V-IIImaj-VI-IImaj-IV-II-I (E-C♯-F♯m-B-D-Bm-A). Notice how I is held back until the very end.
- In traditional theory, II maj and III maj are called secondary dominants.
- **IV min** has a tender, romantic sound. It appears as I-IVm, or as IV-IVm resolving to I or V (C-Dm-F-C-G-F-Fm-C). The Beatles used it constantly. Radiohead's "Creep" is a rock example. For a richer version, try Imaj7-IVm7, or approach IVm from VI.
- Arctic Monkeys sometimes mix reverse-polarity and flat-degree chords. The tip is to be careful doing this within one section, because it can weaken the sense of the home key.

**Other alterations not in the table**
- **Vm** darkens a major key and adds a Mixolydian colour. Examples: "She's Waiting" (I-IV-Vm-IV), "Fly Me Away" (I-Vm-♭VII), "Behind Blue Eyes."
- **♭V** is rare. It can approach V chromatically from IV ("Parklife") or follow I directly ("Richard III," A-E♭).
- **VI maj** creates a strong pull toward a key change. It's used in "Good Day Sunshine," "Goldfinger" (E-C), and "Seen the Light."

**Minor keys.** Find your minor key in column 6, then use columns 1 to 7 plus 10 and 11, renumbered from the minor chord. For A minor: Am, B♭, C, Dm, Em, F, G, plus D and E.
- Natural minor (Aeolian): chords 1 and 3 to 7
- Dorian: the same, but with chord 4 as major (D, not Dm)
- Harmonic minor: make chord 5 major (E)

You can mix both versions of a chord as long as the melody note fits it. A D chord needs F♯ in the melody, while Dm needs F. "She's Not There" alternates Am-D and Am-F. Muse often imply harmonic minor with a major V, as in "Feeling Good" (Gm-Gm/F-E♭-Dsus4-D).

**Key changes.** Find the new key in column 1 and use that row's chords.

**One- and two-chord songs.** One-chord songs are rare, mostly from blues ("Smokestack Lightning") or psychedelia ("Tomorrow Never Knows"). More often a single chord carries just the verse. Two-chord songs include "That's the Way (I Like It)" (Cm-Fm) and "Sex Machine." Making two chords memorable is hard, which is why most songwriters use at least three.
